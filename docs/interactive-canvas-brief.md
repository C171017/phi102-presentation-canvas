# PHI 102 canvas · Story, opening, and audience play

**Creative direction from Howie, November 20, 2026.** The canvas has roughly the shape I want. I want the audience to follow one story from an anime opening through the philosophical argument. I’m leaning toward a single storyline with live quizzes between clips so I can tell whether the audience is following. The premise, the way quizzes are triggered, and much of the later visual design are still open.

## The three parts of the canvas

| Part | Question for the audience | What the canvas develops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Why accept flexible foundationalism? | Where can a chain of reasons stop, and why might it stop somewhere different in a new context? | Introduce contemporary contextualism and flexible foundationalism. Move from the regress problem through Cartesian and moderate foundationalism to the idea that a foundation can be undoubted in this inquiry even though someone could doubt it. Then introduce three routes to flexible foundations. |
| 2. Which version do we get? | When someone calls a belief foundationally justified, whose context matters, and what work does it do? | Revisit the three routes alongside four views of foundational justification. Show what a particular pairing amounts to by playing it out in the story. |
| 3. What does this explain? | What fixes what a character has justification to believe? Can the character tell what they have justification to believe? | Use first-order and higher-order justification to introduce access internalism. Follow what happens to the local supervenience base (the facts that fix what the character has justification to believe) when a new doubt becomes relevant. |

The **three routes** on the canvas are justification as a social activity, hinges, and knowledge or belief at the base (including E = K, the view that one’s evidence consists of what one knows). The **four views** are contextualism, sensitive invariantism, relativism, and expressivism. A route explains how we arrive at flexible foundations. A version develops that idea by specifying how context enters the account. The current canvas works through selected pairings, then uses disagreement to motivate relativism and expressivism.

## One story the audience can play

Keep the goal simple enough to grasp in one shot. The humor can be dry and a little silly: an elaborate anime opening for someone whose entire problem is getting to a party on time. Catching a train would work too. What does the character know about when it leaves? Which facts do they take for granted while checking the timetable? Another premise is welcome if it gives social justification, hinges, and knowledge as evidence a natural place in the action.

**Current story.** Simone takes Echo to his surprise birthday party on the 7:12 train. Evan, Echo’s owner, is hosting across town. His name echoes *evidence*: he signs the invitation in blue ink, and Simone has to decide what she can tell from the smudged hour. In Greco’s original example, the host is Peter and the listener is Justine. [Cast and story](story-and-characters.md)

For example, Simone says, “Evan wrote that it starts at eight.” One group of friends accepts Evan’s word and moves on. Another asks how reliable Evan is. To explore *social justification × contextualism*, the quiz could hold Simone’s evidence fixed and ask what changes when a speaker from each group says, “Simone is justified in taking Evan’s word for granted.” Later, the application could return to the same line by asking what Simone could reasonably believe if part of the hour had smudged. That gives the audience a reason to reconsider which facts fix what Simone should believe.

The purple crystal could connect the opening, the argument, and the game as a single recurring asset. It might be something the characters are looking for, or a magical object they already possess. Its role in the plot is undecided.

## Anime opening (OP)

Make a fan-made opening in homage to the *Cowboy Bebop* OP. Use Higgsfeld and image and video generation to develop it. The opening should set the tone, foreshadow the argument, and give the audience enough of the character’s situation to step into the first scene. Plan for three kinds of on-screen text:

| Text | Job in the opening |
| --- | --- |
| Credits and acknowledgments | Identify the people behind the presentation, including the producer or team and the professor as appropriate. Confirm names and roles before production. |
| Philosophical words and phrases | Flash terms such as *context*, *hinges*, and *flexible foundationalism* as clues to what is coming. |
| Story text | Let successive frames establish who the character is, what they want, and what they are unsure about. In the party example, one frame could show Simone heading out and the next could reveal the doubt about the start time. |

## Live quizzes and progression

**Preferred direction.** Give the class one story they can finish in one sitting. The audience will probably see the presentation only once. A shared sequence lets everyone see the payoff, while the votes tell the presenter which ideas may need another example.

1. **Trigger the quiz.** The presenter could activate it when the room is ready, or it could appear automatically on reaching a designated canvas page. The trigger method is open.
2. **Put one moment from the story to a vote.** Show a short scenario, one comprehension question, three or four options, and a visible timer. Everyone can answer. For a hinge question, Simone and a friend might agree the invitation is genuine while debating whether “8” means morning or evening. Ask which assumption they are holding fixed as they investigate. Other checkpoints can ask whose objections Simone needs to answer or what counts as evidence under E = K.
3. **Reveal the result.** When voting closes, show which option received the most votes. Always reveal the correct answer, even if another option won. A sentence connecting the answer to the scene gives the presenter a chance to clear up confusion before continuing.
4. **Play the next story clip.** Completing the question and reveal moves the plot forward, including when the class picked the wrong answer. Plan for a sequence of short Higgsfeld-generated clips throughout the presentation, alongside the opening. Each clip picks up where the last scenario left off.

**Alternative still under consideration.** The option with the most votes could determine a story consequence, sending the class down one of several connected paths with different endings. The single-story approach above is the current preference because a live audience is unlikely to replay the other paths.

The next design pass can settle the premise, the crystal’s role, and the moments that deserve a quiz and a follow-up clip.
